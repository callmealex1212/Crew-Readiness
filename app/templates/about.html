Privacy & limits

Privacy, science and limits

What this prototype is, what it is not, and how it handles data.

01What it does

It detects changes in task interaction timing compared with each person's own usual pattern, and flags changes that may merit a human check-in before a critical operation.

02What it does not do
  • It does not detect or diagnose fatigue, illness, or cognitive impairment.
  • It does not clear anyone for duty or control any mission decision.
  • Its thresholds are illustrative prototype choices, not NASA-approved or clinically validated limits.
03Data handling
  • Only aggregate timing numbers are stored (see the preview shown before every submission).
  • Typed text, key identities and raw keystroke timelines never leave the browser. The server rejects them.
  • Monitoring runs only inside the task, with a visible indicator.
  • Demo crew members are fictional; their data is synthetic and labelled as such.
04Retention & access

Proposal for any deployment, not a feature of this prototype: agree a short, documented retention period before collection; limit access to the crew member and named human reviewers with a need to review a flagged change; and let a reviewer remove or accept a new normal with a visible audit record. The prototype has no accounts. When a reviewer accepts a new normal, it records a timestamp, not who accepted it.

05Context matters

A timing change can have ordinary explanations. Keyboard and device changes, language and task familiarity, time of day, sleep, and practice effect can all change task timing. A flag is a prompt for a human check-in, not an explanation of its cause.

06What we validated

On simulated data only, we compared the current pause-count model with the earlier median/MAD approach using the same synthetic crews: 300 simulated people per row, with 40 sessions each. At the shipped alert level, normal-session false alerts fell from about 13.0% to about 4.7%; for a simulated 10% slowdown, affected sessions flagged fell from 0.42 to 0.35. At a matched 5% false-alert rate, the current model detected more of the simulated changes tested. These are a reproducible trade-off in our simulator, not evidence about real crews.

We also simulated sustained changes. Keeping flagged sessions out of the baseline stopped a 20% simulated slowdown from being rapidly learned as normal. A human can accept a genuine new normal, but that human decision was not benchmarked.

07What we did not validate
  • No real people: we have no data from astronauts, crews, volunteers, or any other real participants.
  • No suitable licensed public dataset: we found none with enough repeated sessions per person and a licence we could cite.
  • Our simulator and model were both written by us. Several burstiness levels and a wrong-assumption test reduce, but do not remove, that circularity.
  • Total pause time is displayed as context but is not scored, so one very long pause can go unflagged.
  • Thresholds are illustrative. The prototype does not detect fatigue or illness, determine fitness for duty, or have NASA approval or endorsement.
